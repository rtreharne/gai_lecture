# AI as a Bioscience Superpower — revised 20-slide blueprint

Presenter: **Dr. Robert Treharne**, R.Treharne@liverpool.ac.uk  
Audience: first-year bioscience students, approximately 150–300  
Duration: **45 minutes** · Website: **exactly 20 slides**  
Delivery: paper speaking material; no website presenter-notes interface.

## What changed

The lecture now follows one sequence: understand the tool → see it used in research → practise learning with it → apply it to careers → make responsible choices. Related topics and their polls share slides. Each scientific example explains a question, what the model contributes and what still needs testing.

BIOS105 assessment-tier content, course-specific tier claims, penalties and tier quizzes have been removed. One short general-use reminder remains before the live demo. The separate School policy audit is unchanged.

The tone stays optimistic, with roughly 70% of the time on capability, learning and opportunity. Greater depth comes from worked examples and audience reasoning, not more text on screen.

## Outcomes

Students should be able to explain a useful capability and a limitation of GenAI; distinguish a model prediction from experimental evidence; practise an answer–feedback–retry study loop; map real experience to a job requirement; and choose a practical next step while checking instructions, evidence and privacy.

## Slide-by-slide delivery

### 1. AI as a Bioscience Superpower (0–2 min)

Combine the opening hook, lecturer introduction and first word cloud. Spend 45 seconds collecting answers, then connect two responses to learning, discovery or careers. Transition: “To use this well, it helps to know what the tool is doing.”

**Student-facing message:** A patient tutor. A faster way through routine work. A partner for exploring new ideas.

**Interaction:** What would you most like AI to help you do?

Word cloud or call out one idea

### 2. How did we get from rules to conversation? (2–4 min)

Explain the shift from hand-written rules to patterns learned from data. Cover Turing, Dartmouth, ELIZA, expert systems and AI winters briefly; spend most time on deep learning, transformers and the move to text, images and scientific sequences. Define a foundation model as a broadly trained model that can be adapted to different tasks. Avoid implying all AI is generative.

**Student-facing message:** Earlier systems followed rules written by people. Modern models learn patterns from examples, then generate new text, images or sequences.

### 3. A convincing answer still needs checking. (4–6 min)

Use the reference question to explain why confident wording is not a truth guarantee. Ask the room how they would verify a citation: locate the actual paper, read the relevant passage and check that it supports the claim. Asking the same model “are you sure?” is not independent verification. Transition from model limitations to the questions fiction anticipated.

- **Why it helps:** It can connect ideas, change the level of an explanation and offer useful starting points.
- **Why it can fail:** It can produce a plausible answer even when a fact, source or assumption is wrong.

**Interaction:** An AI gives you a reference. What would you check first?

Find the original source, then check whether it supports the claim.

### 4. We imagined the possibilities. Now we get to make choices. (6–8 min)

Use three themes rather than a quiz about book titles. The Diamond Age asks what personalised education might offer; I, Robot and 2001 ask how rules and objectives can fail; Dick and Card explore empathy and the consequences of simulation. Keep the longer reading list as paper material. Transition: “In bioscience, these possibilities are already becoming practical tools.”

**Student-facing message:** Read these as questions about our future, rather than predictions of how models work.

- **A tutor for everyone?:** The Diamond Age — personalised teaching, with human relationships still central.
- **A system we can trust?:** I, Robot and 2001 — helpful rules, conflicting goals and unexpected consequences.
- **What makes us human?:** Philip K. Dick — empathy and authenticity. Ender’s Game — learning through simulation.

### 5. Which molecule should we test next? (8–11 min)

Work through a single research problem: finding a molecule that interacts with a target. Explain the difference between predicting a complex and designing a new candidate. AlphaFold 3 is a peer-reviewed structure-prediction case; it does not establish affinity, biological activity or safety by itself. Show the propose–prioritise–make–measure cycle. Ask what evidence would convince students a candidate actually works. Avoid presenting a generic generated protein as experimentally validated.

**Student-facing message:** AI helps narrow a huge search to a smaller set of promising experiments.

- **Predict a structure:** AlphaFold 3 models complexes involving proteins, DNA, RNA and small molecules. A predicted interaction gives researchers something to investigate.
- **Design a candidate:** Generative protein models propose new sequences. Researchers still need to make them and test binding, activity and safety.

### 6. What might this change do to a living system? (11–14 min)

Connect the three scales: DNA sequence, cell behaviour, and images of cells. Explain gene-expression profiles as measurements of which genes are active. For Evo 2, say what the cited manuscript investigates rather than claiming all generated sequences function in organisms. For single-cell models, describe useful learned patterns without suggesting they simulate a complete cell. Virtual staining produces inferred image content; compare it with experimental measurements. AI co-scientists propose and rank hypotheses, with researchers testing them. These are examples of computational support, with validation specific to each study.

**Student-facing message:** A simulated cell, generated image or promising hypothesis is a model output. Compare it with real measurements.

- **A change in DNA:** Evo 2 learns genomic patterns to help predict effects of sequence changes and propose sequences for testing.
- **A change in a cell:** Single-cell models learn from gene-expression profiles. Researchers explore how those patterns help identify cell states and responses.
- **A new way to see:** Generative microscopy can create virtual stains. AI co-scientists suggest hypotheses; experiments establish which ideas hold up.

### 7. Let AI help you work things out. (14–16 min)

Consolidate tutor, coach, assistant and collaborator around the situations a student recognises. Give concrete follow-ups: “Try a different analogy; where does it break down?” and “Ask me a harder question about the same idea.” Include accessibility: repetition, flexible pacing and explanation styles. Take a quick vote before moving into the demonstration.

- **When you’re stuck:** Ask for a simpler explanation, then an example and a question to check your understanding.
- **When you’re revising:** Ask for one question at a time. Try an answer before seeing feedback.
- **When work is repetitive:** Ask for a table, a timetable draft or help understanding a code error. Check the result.

**Interaction:** Which would help you most this week: explanations, practice or organisation?

Quick vote · patient repetition and different explanation styles can improve access.

### 8. Three habits for responsible use. (16–18 min)

Keep just this two-minute general-use reminder. No BIOS105 tier labels, tier table, course-specific assessment claims, deduction percentages or tier quizzes. Students check the current assessment or employer instructions, use approved tools, protect personal/confidential/teaching material and follow declaration requirements. This is preparation for the demos, not a complete policy training session. The independent policy audit remains a separate reference.

**Student-facing message:** Practising with an AI tutor does not automatically give permission to use it in an assessment.

- **Check the instructions:** For assessments, read the brief and ask if you are unsure. For applications, check the employer’s rules.
- **Choose what you share:** Use approved University tools. Keep personal, confidential and protected teaching material out of prompts.
- **Be open about your use:** Follow declaration requirements. Keep enough detail to explain what the tool did and what you checked.

### 9. “Help me understand it. Then test me.” (18–22 min)

Run the four-minute tutor demonstration below. Let the class attempt the first question before submitting the misconception. Show feedback, ask for a hint or new example, then close the chat and invite an independent explanation. Verify one factual claim using a trusted textbook. If the service is slow, use the clearly labelled illustrative exchange below on paper. Do not add fallback slides to the 20-slide count.

**Student-facing message:** Example misconception: “Adding more substrate cannot help if an inhibitor is present.”

**On-screen prompt:**

> Quiz me on competitive enzyme inhibition. Ask one question at a time and wait for my attempt. Give a hint if I’m stuck. After feedback, ask a new question that tests the same idea.

**Interaction:** What would you change in that explanation?

Answer together · verify the correction against a trusted textbook.

### 10. Automate the routine. Keep the practice. (22–24 min)

Ask the classification question before opening the answer reveal. Discuss why proposing possible explanations can help, but choosing a conclusion requires evidence and context. For low-value transformations, checking is still needed. The principle is to decide which skill an activity is supposed to develop before outsourcing any of it.

- **Let it help:** Reformat a table, generate quiz variants or explain an error message. Verify the output.
- **Do the thinking:** Interpret an unexpected result, choose a control or explain why your conclusion follows.

**Interaction:** Your experiment gives an unexpected result. Should AI decide the explanation, suggest possibilities, or be avoided entirely?



**Reveal after discussion:** Use it to suggest possibilities. You choose what fits the evidence and which experiment could distinguish the explanations.

### 11. Could you still do it without the screen? (24–26 min)

Use WALL-E as an accessible metaphor for dependence. Explain recognition versus recall with a concrete test: recognising an enzyme explanation differs from drawing or applying it unaided. Give pairs 30 seconds to name a skill they want to retain. Recommend attempt-first, hints, explanation aloud and occasional AI-free practice. Keep the tone encouraging: tools should make the learner more capable.

**Student-facing message:** In WALL-E, convenience has made people dependent on systems they barely understand. That is a useful warning for learning.

- **The trap:** A clear explanation feels familiar, so you assume you could reproduce it. Confidence grows faster than understanding.
- **The habit:** Close the chat. Explain the idea aloud, sketch the mechanism or solve a fresh problem. Revisit the gap you find.

**Interaction:** What is one skill you want to keep practising yourself?

30 seconds with the person next to you.

### 12. Build skills that work together. (26–28 min)

Introduce the three skill sets with examples: biology and practical competence; AI/data skills and checking; judgement, communication and teamwork. A job contains tasks that may change in different ways. WEF employer surveys and ILO exposure studies inform possibilities rather than guarantee individual job outcomes. Transition: “How do you turn those skills into opportunities?”

**Student-facing message:** Understand the biology. Use tools thoughtfully. Explain your decisions.

### 13. Turn “I like biology” into a next step. (28–30 min)

Demonstrate a continuous career workflow: explore families of roles; decode a real advert; audit real evidence; plan a gap; prepare and rehearse. Explain that adverts and human conversations are how students verify AI suggestions. Use Handshake and the Career Studio as next steps. Collect the quick skill vote without making a separate poll slide.

- **Start with a question:** “I enjoy microbiology and explaining science. What kinds of roles could I explore, and where can I verify them?”
- **Follow up with people:** Use real vacancies, Handshake, the Career Studio and conversations with working scientists.

**Interaction:** Which skill would you like evidence of by the end of this year?

Lab skills · data analysis · communication · teamwork · AI literacy

### 14. A better application starts with real evidence. (30–34 min)

Run the four-minute careers demo below. Map the same fictional student to the same role throughout. A café example can support communication or teamwork, but it does not prove laboratory competence. Missing coding experience is a development opportunity. Ask for questions that elicit a STAR example: situation, task, action, result. Students must be able to explain every claim in an application. Employer rules still apply.

**Student-facing message:** Improve the explanation of your experience. Never invent the experience.

- **The vacancy:** Research assistant: accurate records, basic data analysis, clear communication. PCR or coding desirable.
- **The student:** Practical work in pairs, graphs, a group poster and a weekend café job. No coding experience yet.

**On-screen prompt:**

> Match only my real experience to this advert. Show evidence, partial evidence and gaps. Suggest three achievable first-year actions. Ask questions to help me prepare an honest interview example.

### 15. Make a small project. Explain your choices. (34–36 min)

Turn employability advice into a manageable first-year project. A public dataset could become a reproducible graph with a short explanation; a revision resource could include checked questions and corrections. Ask students to record the question, method, AI contribution, checks, result and limitation. Do not promise that a small project guarantees a job. The value is concrete evidence of skills and reflection.

**Student-facing message:** A small, honest example gives you something concrete to discuss with an employer.

- **Pick a manageable task:** Use a public biology dataset, or create a revision resource from your own notes.
- **Keep a clear record:** Save your question, method, AI contribution and checks. Show a useful result and one limitation.
- **Tell the story:** What did you do? Why did you choose it? How did you check it? What would you improve?

### 16. Useful tools have real-world costs. (36–38 min)

Explain direct electricity use and cooling-related water demand without repeating universal per-prompt statistics. Include people doing annotation and moderation, unequal access and the limits of model coverage. Discuss benefits alongside costs: research, medicine and optimisation may be valuable, but benefit should be evaluated rather than assumed. A purposeful task and proportional tool choice are practical starting points.

**Student-facing message:** AI can also support medicine, energy optimisation and access to expertise. Benefits and costs need to be assessed together.

- **Electricity + water:** Computing uses power; cooling can use water. Impacts depend on the model, location and infrastructure.
- **People + access:** People label data and moderate content. Costs, language coverage and biased outputs affect who benefits.
- **Use with purpose:** Match the tool to the task, reuse useful results and ask providers for better evidence about their impacts.

### 17. Who benefits? Who gets to decide? (38–40 min)

Focus the ownership discussion on control of compute, capital, access and incentives. Use FTC/CMA evidence as a basis, without turning the slide into an attack on identity or personalities. Take a 45-second vote on principles and one reason from the room. A useful example is the tension between low-cost access and a provider's commercial incentive to gather more data.

**Student-facing message:** A small group of companies controls much of the computing, investment and access. Their incentives help shape which problems get solved.

**Interaction:** What matters most to you: sustainability, fair access, privacy, transparency, or human accountability?

Rank or vote · explain one trade-off

### 18. Two situations. What would you do? (40–42 min)

Run the two checks with answers initially hidden. Poll A: plausible reference—A cite immediately, B check original and support, C ask the same model if sure. Answer B. Poll B: missing job skill—A invent evidence, B obscure the gap, C identify honest evidence or a development plan. Answer C. Allow 20 seconds per question, then reveal and explain. This replaces the former tier-based exit test.

- **A reference looks convincing:** A: cite it immediately. B: find the original and check the claim. C: ask the same model if it is sure.
- **A job asks for a skill you lack:** A: let AI invent an example. B: hide the gap with polished wording. C: identify an honest example or plan how to develop the skill.

**Reveal after discussion:** Reference: B. Career: C. Verify the evidence, be honest about what you can do, and check the task or employer’s instructions.

### 19. Choose one useful thing to try this week. (42–44 min)

Give students a full minute to choose a specific action and write when they will try it. Invite one study action and one career action. Ask them to state how they will check whether the tool helped. Success is one concrete next step, not another list of tips.

- **Learn:** Try the answer–feedback–retry loop on a topic you find difficult.
- **Save time:** Automate one repetitive task and check the result.
- **Explore:** Find one real vacancy and identify a skill you could start building.

**Interaction:** Which will you try, and when?

Write down one action · make it small enough to start.

### 20. More possibilities. Your judgement. (44–45 min)

Close with the optional BioBoost invitation and the presenter's name and email. Retain [BIOBOOST_URL] until supplied; do not claim a credential, duration, launch date or booking process. Echo the opening: more opportunities to learn, solve problems and explore careers, with the student's judgement central.

**Student-facing message:** Want guided practice using AI for study, problem-solving and future opportunities? Continue with BioBoost | GAI Foundations.

**On-screen prompt:**

> [BIOBOOST_URL]

## Paper demonstration scripts

### Slide 9: learning tutor, four minutes

Use University Copilot with a clean chat. Use the standalone concept below, without uploading slides, rubrics, assessed work or identifying information.

**0:00–0:30 — prompt**

> Quiz me on competitive enzyme inhibition. Ask one question at a time and wait for my attempt. Give a hint if I’m stuck. After feedback, ask a new question that tests the same idea.

**0:30–1:30 — class attempt**

Read the model's first question aloud. Let the room try. If useful, introduce this misconception: “Adding more substrate cannot help if an inhibitor is present.” Ask students what they would change.

**1:30–2:30 — feedback and transfer**

> Give me a short explanation and an analogy. Tell me one way the analogy is misleading. Then ask a new question without revealing its answer.

**2:30–4:00 — verification and independent explanation**

Check one claim against the chosen trusted textbook/public source. Close the chat and ask a student to explain the mechanism. Under the standard competitive-inhibition model, apparent Km increases while Vmax is unchanged. Do not generalise this statement to all inhibitor mechanisms.

**Illustrative fallback, not a captured Copilot result:** Tutor asks whether more substrate changes the chance of substrate rather than inhibitor occupying the active site. Student predicts that more substrate can outcompete the inhibitor. Tutor asks what that implies for the maximum rate at sufficiently high substrate. Use this exchange on paper if the live service is unavailable.

### Slide 14: careers coach, four minutes

This is a fictional teaching vacancy, not a current advertised opportunity.

**Vacancy**

> Graduate Research Assistant, Molecular Biology. Maintain accurate laboratory records, prepare samples, follow SOPs and safety procedures, analyse results in spreadsheets, and communicate with a team. Essential: a relevant degree in progress or completed, attention to detail and quantitative and communication skills. Desirable: PCR, cell culture, R or Python.

**Fictional student**

> I have completed practical work in pairs, recorded measurements and graphed results. I contributed to a group poster and work weekends in a café. I have not learned coding yet.

**0:00–1:00 — decode**

> Separate essential and desirable skills in this vacancy. Match only my actual experience to them. Label each match evidence, partial evidence or gap. Do not invent any experience.

**1:00–2:00 — critique**

Ask the room whether café work demonstrates lab skills or communication. Discuss what extra detail is needed before claiming teamwork or data-analysis proficiency.

**2:00–3:00 — plan**

> Suggest three achievable first-year development actions: one data skill, one practical or project opportunity, and one conversation with a real person. Treat these as ideas to verify, not guaranteed opportunities.

**3:00–4:00 — rehearse**

> Ask me questions to build a STAR example from one experience. Ask what I personally did and how I know it helped. Leave unknown details blank.

**Illustrative fallback:** graphing measurements = partial evidence for data analysis; group poster = possible communication evidence needing personal contribution; no coding = gap. Actions: practise a simple public-data analysis, seek an appropriate practical/project opportunity, and talk to the Career Studio. Do not turn the gaps into invented achievements.
